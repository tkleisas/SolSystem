# The setting

The world of SolSystem: what happened, who is here, and what is at stake. The physics and
economics are derived in `docs/TRIP-ENERGY.md`; this is the history and the biology.

Status markers as in `DESIGN.md`: **[DECIDED]**, **[OPEN]**, **[DEFERRED]**.

---

## 1. Three worlds, one origin

**[DECIDED] Life arose on all three of Earth, Mars and Venus.** Not independently — the
same lineage, seeded across the inner system during the late heavy bombardment, when all
three were warm and wet and the young Sun was dim enough for Venus to hold an ocean.

For a billion years there were three inhabited worlds. Then two of them died, for different
reasons and on different timescales, and only Earth was left.

| | Why it lived or died |
|---|---|
| **Earth** | Large enough to hold its atmosphere and its magnetic field. Still alive. |
| **Mars** | Too small. Lost its magnetic field, then its air, then its water, then everything. |
| **Venus** | Right size, wrong distance. A runaway greenhouse boiled the oceans and cooked the surface. |

**Earth is the third attempt, and it is failing too.** That is the whole premise: not "we
found a new home" but "we are the last of three, and we are running out of time."

This is also why nobody in this setting is surprised to find traces of life elsewhere. The
traces are family.

---

## 2. Mars — a once-living world, and what is left of it

**[DECIDED] Mars was inhabited, and it was inhabited by something that thought.**

Mars had an ocean, a thick atmosphere and a magnetic field for perhaps a billion years.
Life took hold there as it did on Earth. Then the dynamo stopped, the air was stripped, and
the surface froze and dried over a span of hundreds of millions of years — slow enough that
whatever lived there had time to see it coming and no way to stop it.

What remains:

- **Fossils** in the layered sediments of the northern basin, abundant enough that the
  evidence is not in question. Microbial, mostly. Enough.
- **Structures.** A handful of sites that are not explained by geology: cut stone at
  scales that imply organisation, and — the part that changed everything — **a single
  installation under the south polar deposits that is unmistakably engineered.** Not a
  ruin. A *sealed* facility, built to last, and it did.
- **Records.** Partial, damaged, written in a system nobody has fully decoded. Enough has
  been read to know two things: they knew they were dying, and **they tried to leave.**

**[DECIDED] They did not leave.** No trace of them has ever been found anywhere else in the
system. Not on Earth, not in the belt, not outward. Whatever the south polar facility was
for, it did not save them.

### Why this matters to the war

**The Illuminus are excavating it.** This is the strongest card in the setting and it costs
nothing:

The Illuminus' entire project — mind transfer, shells, the abandonment of the body — is an
attempt to survive by ceasing to be biological. They are doing this on a planet where
**somebody already tried exactly that, and failed, and left the records behind.**

The obvious questions follow immediately, and they are all unpleasant:

1. **What killed them?** Their star was stable and their planet's decline was slow. If a
   civilisation with a billion years of head start could not outlast its own world, what
   makes the Illuminus think a few centuries of shells will do it?
2. **Did they upload?** If the south polar facility contains a working mind-transfer
   technology — or the wreckage of one — then the Illuminus are not inventing their
   salvation, they are *re-deriving somebody else's failure.*
3. **Did it work and then stop?** The worst possibility, and the one the Illuminus do not
   discuss: that they succeeded, and the machines are still down there, dormant, and that
   the record simply ends.

**[OPEN] What is actually in the south polar facility, and how much of it the Illuminus
have opened.** This is the setting's central mystery and the natural spine of a campaign.
Options range from a warning to a working artefact to something still running.

**[OPEN] Why they could not leave.** They had a billion years and a whole solar system to
reach. The answer shapes whether Mars is a tomb or a warning — and whether humanity's own
expansion is repeating a mistake or avoiding one.

## 3. Venus — the cloud hives

**[DECIDED] Venus has its own biosphere, and it is not related to Earth's.**

Earth life is carbon–oxygen chemistry in water. Venus's cloud life is **carbon–sulfur
chemistry** — a genuinely different biochemistry, and the second independent origin in the
solar system. Where Earth's biology runs on water and oxygen, this runs on **sulfur
allotropes, carbon disulfide and carbonyl sulfide**, cycling through the cloud deck's
sulfuric acid aerosols.

It is real chemistry, not magic. Sulfur forms long chains and rings much as carbon does,
carbon disulfide is a stable solvent, and Venus's clouds contain exactly the sulfur species
a metabolism would need. The 2020s phosphine detections were the first argument that
something in those clouds was not in equilibrium. They were right, and it took a century to
prove it.

### What they are

**[DECIDED] Hive organisms.** Not individual animals but **colonies** — floating mats and
bladders at 50–60 km altitude, kilometres across at the largest, which is the only reason
they were found at all. Individually they are small; collectively they are the largest
living things in the solar system.

They are **slow**. A hive's metabolic cycle is measured in years, and its "thought", if it
is thought, is slower still. They have been drifting in those clouds for longer than
multicellular life has existed on Earth.

**[OPEN] Are they intelligent?** This is the setting's hardest question and the one that
decides the Workers' moral position. Three defensible answers:

- **No** — they are ecosystems, extraordinary but mindless. The question is ecological.
- **Yes, and slowly** — they think at a pace where a human lifetime is one exchange. They
  cannot be talked to, only *consulted*, over decades.
- **They are one thing** — not a species but a single distributed organism, and it has been
  aware of the ships in its sky for two hundred years.

### Why this matters to the war

**The Workers are dissolving the atmosphere these things live in.**

Terraforming Venus means removing 99 % of the CO₂ — stripping the cloud deck out of
existence over a few centuries. The hives live *in the thing being dismantled.* There is no
version of the Workers' project that leaves the hives where they are.

So the Workers' own ideology turns on them. They are pro-equality, anti-hierarchy,
committed to the proposition that a mind is a mind regardless of what it is made of. **And
they are exterminating the only other living thing in the solar system, because they need
the sky it lives in.**

That is a real dilemma with no clean answer, and it is far more interesting than a
faction that is simply right:

- The Workers' official position is that the hives are **not minds** — that a slow chemical
  colony is not a person, and that humanity's survival outweighs an ecosystem. They say it
  carefully, and they believe it, and it is exactly the argument the Illuminus make about
  shells.
- A minority in the Workers argue for **preservation** — lifting samples, maintaining a
  cloud-deck reserve, or simply *waiting* to find out whether the hives are people. They are
  losing the argument, because the clock in §1 does not stop.
- **The Illuminus do not care either way**, and say so. They find the Workers' discomfort
  amusing and their hesitation useful. If the hives can be *used* — as a chemical factory, a
  weapon, or a bargaining chip — the Illuminus will use them.

**[OPEN] What the hives do when the terraforming starts.** Whether they can be preserved,
whether they respond, and whether they are already adapting. A hive that is *reacting* to
the swarm would transform the Workers' project from engineering into a war they did not know
they were fighting.

## 4. What this changes about the factions

**[DECIDED] Neither faction is wrong, and neither is right.**

| | Workers | Illuminus |
|---|---|---|
| Project | Terraform Venus — the world their bodies fit | Terraform Mars — the world their bodies fit |
| Ideology | Equality, production, the many | Hierarchy, inheritance, the few |
| Their crime | Dissolving a biosphere to survive | Turning people into property to survive |
| Their justification | "A slow chemical colony is not a mind." | "A body is not a person." |
| **Both are arguing the same thing** | *That something alive does not count.* | |

That symmetry is the point. **Both factions have decided that some living thing does not
qualify as a person, and both are acting on it.** The Workers apply it to the hives; the
Illuminus apply it to the bodies they discard and the copies they cull. Neither can afford
to admit the parallel, and the scoundrel — who belongs to neither and needs both — can see
it plainly.

**[DECIDED] And Mars is the warning that neither is listening to.** A civilisation on Mars
had a billion years, tried everything, and left nothing but a sealed door. Both factions
study it. Both conclude it means *their* plan is the one that works.

## 5. Timeline

**[DECIDED] The game opens in 2185.** Everything below is dated to that, and the two
constraints that fix the rest are the Exchange and the divergence.

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| — | **Deep past.** Three living worlds. Martian life reaches complexity and then a technological civilisation; Venusian life persists in the clouds. |
| — | **The Martian end.** Prolonged, foreseen, unstoppable. Everything they built is gone except the sealed facility. |
| 1990–2040 | **Human expansion.** Earth reaches the belt, then Mars. The first closed habitats. The Jovian system is mapped and claimed, not settled. |
| 2062 | **Mars is settled to stay.** The population stops being a crew and starts being a people. |
| 2071 | **First Venus cloud city.** At 55 km the pressure is 0.53 bar and the temperature 27 °C, which turns out to be the easiest place in the solar system to keep humans alive outside Earth. |
| 2081 | **The hives are found.** First the phosphine, then the colonies. The argument about what they are has been running for a century by the time the game starts. |
| **2090** | **The Exchange.** A limited nuclear war that kills nine in ten humans. See §5.1. |
| 2090–2120 | **The long silence.** Earth is quarantined by its own collapse rather than by policy. The off-world settlements lose their industrial base and most of their people. |
| 2120–2185 | **The divergence.** Mars-adapted and Venus-adapted populations separate biologically over three to four generations, and stop being able to live on each other's worlds. |
| **2185** | **The south polar door.** The Illuminus open it. Whatever happens next is the game. |

### 5.1 The Exchange

**[DECIDED] Ninety per cent of humanity died.** The number is not flavour, it is the
arithmetic the whole setting rests on, so it is written down with its parts:

| | Before, 2090 | After |
|---|---|---|
| Earth | 10.6 billion | 1.06 billion |
| Off-world — belt, Mars, orbital habitats, Jovian stations | 400 million | 68 million |
| **Total** | **11.0 billion** | **1.1 billion** |

The direct exchange killed a fraction of that. Most of it was what came after: a
decade of nuclear winter collapsing agriculture worldwide, and then the collapse of
everything that keeps a technological civilisation alive — medicine, spare parts,
refrigeration, the supply chains that move food from where it grows to where people
are. **Earth died of the war. The settlements died of losing Earth**, which they
depended on for equipment they could not yet make and could not do without.

> **The off-world population lost 83 % and not one warhead was aimed at it.** That is
> the shape of the catastrophe: the people who thought they had escaped were the ones
> who starved when the place they had escaped from stopped answering.

**[DECIDED] Some had already left.** The expansion of the 2030s–2050s was not
evacuation and never pretended to be; it was industry, science and claim-jumping. But
it means the off-world settlements were *founded before the exchange*, by people who
chose to go, which is why they exist at all and why they carry the question with them:
**we left, and then we watched.**

**By 2185 the off-world population is about 95 million** — 80 million Workers, 2
million Illuminus, 13 million elsewhere. That is 0.5 % a year of growth from 68 million
over ninety-five years, which is what recovering from a catastrophe looks like rather
than a heroic figure. **Earth's survivors number around 900 million and are declining**,
so the people watching Earth die are a small splinter of humanity who left, and the
people dying are the vast majority who could not.

### 5.2 Who started it — the open wound

**[DECIDED] Nobody in 2185 knows, and the question cannot be closed.** This is a plot
point rather than a mystery to be solved, and it works because of one fact: **the
exchange destroyed both homelands.** Today's population descends from the bombed and
from the bombers, mixed, because there is no longer any difference between them. So the
question "who started it" is not a question about other people. It is unanswerable *and*
unavoidable, and every faction has built a story that happens to serve it.

**[DECIDED] The dominant off-world account blames Earth.** The pre-war settlements were
taxed, licenced and held to a launch monopoly by the states that had built them, and the
settlements had become worth more than their founders. The story runs: Earth's biosphere
was failing, Earth demanded the belt's output outright to pay for a rescue it had no plan
to perform, and when the settlements refused, Earth moved to take what it needed. **This
is the Workers' account**, and it is popular with anyone who has ever been governed from
somewhere they have never seen.

**[DECIDED] The dominant Earth-side account blames the settlements.** The settlements
had the resources and the leverage, and they used it — holding prices, withholding
supply, and finally declaring independence at the exact moment Earth could least afford
it. The story runs: a handful of people who had escaped the gravity well decided the
people still under it could pay. **This is the Illuminus' account**, and it is popular
with anyone who has been left behind.

**Both accounts contain things that are true.** The settlements were squeezed and they
did withhold. The launch monopoly was real and was used. Neither account requires any
evidence, which is the point: a century of records burned in an afternoon, and the
survivors were not thinking about archives.

> **What neither account wants to say** is that the escalation was not controlled by
> whoever decided to start it. Somebody launched first, believing a limited strike would
> force a negotiation, and the negotiation never came, because the systems built to
> guarantee retaliation had been built to work when nobody was left to give the order.
> Both stories are about a *decision*, because a decision can be blamed on a person.
> The truth is closer to nobody deciding anything after the first afternoon.

**[OPEN]** Whether the game ever confirms the real sequence. It is usable three ways
and the choice is a tone decision: **never resolved** (both factions stay right and the
player has to live with it), **resolved in the sealed facility** (the Martians watched
the whole thing and recorded it, which makes the campaign's spine carry the answer), or
**resolved as a lie** (a document surfaces, one faction publishes it, and it is a
forgery — the reaction to it is the story rather than the document).

### 5.3 What it does to the setting

**[DECIDED] Earth is neutral because it is powerless, not because it is virtuous.**
Nine hundred million people on a dying planet with no launch capacity and no fleet do
not get a vote. The factions' respect for Earth's neutrality is real and is also a
courtesy they can withdraw at any time, which is exactly §1.3's "Earth becomes a live
battlefield" waiting to happen.

**[DECIDED] The cull is the Exchange at small scale.** Illuminus society votes,
ceremonially and in public, on which of its own minds to destroy. They did not invent
this. They are a hundred years downstream of a catastrophe in which a small number of
people decided which of everyone else would live, and they have built a government that
does it on a schedule. **They are not repeating the Exchange out of malice; they are
repeating it because it is the only model of hard decisions they inherited.**

**[DECIDED] The Workers' founding promise is the one they cannot keep.** Their
egalitarianism comes out of the habitats, where air and water were shared because they
had to be, and their position is that nobody gets left behind. Earth is nine hundred
million people who were left behind. The Workers are the faction that says this is
intolerable, and they are also the faction that cannot fix it, because by §15 of
`docs/TRIP-ENERGY.md` the transport to evacuate so much as a million of them does not
exist. **Their ideology is not hypocrisy. It is a promise made by people who have
already broken it once.**

**[DECIDED] Which is why the war is the same war.** §4's scarcity is not a new problem.
It is the problem that killed Earth, arriving a second time between people who now have
no shared planet to fall back on — and both factions know it, which is why neither will
say it out loud.
